\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

We evaluate our proposed algorithms using real-world request traces to answer three key questions. First, what is the \textbf{economic potential} of optimal routing compared to naive baselines (Stage 1)? Second, when does \textbf{concurrency become a bottleneck}, and what is the ``cost of concurrency'' (Stage 2)? Third, can our learning-augmented algorithm approach the \textbf{offline optimal} performance under uncertainty?

\subsection{Experimental Setup}

\paragraph{Datasets.} We use three real-world datasets with distinct characteristics, plus one synthetic trace for scalability evaluation, summarized in Table~\ref{tab:datasets}. \textbf{ShareGPT} contains 201K requests from single-turn conversations, representing a standard chatbot workload with moderate output lengths. \textbf{FreeInference} contains 371K requests with a multi-model mix, representing a diverse API gateway workload with high variance in both input and output. \textbf{Enterprise} is an anonymized workload collected from a production coding assistant deployment, exhibiting long contexts and the highest cost variance (coefficient of variation $> 2$). \textbf{BurstGPT} is a synthetic trace combining arrival timestamps from BurstGPT with prompt distributions from ShareGPT, yielding 1.4M requests for online algorithm evaluation at scale.

\paragraph{Baselines.} We compare against three baselines. \textbf{All-API} routes all requests to the pay-per-token API, serving as the cost upper bound. \textbf{Greedy (FCFS)} routes to subscription whenever capacity is available, regardless of request value. \textbf{Optimal (Offline)} computes the theoretical lower bound via our Stage 1/2 oracles.

\paragraph{Pricing Model.} We use the pricing of DeepSeek-R1 as a representative model: \$0.55/1M input tokens and \$2.19/1M output tokens. The subscription cost is amortized to define the break-even point.

\subsection{Predictor Calibration Analysis}

To understand why the simpler EMA predictor outperforms the histogram-based quantile predictor, we analyze their calibration properties. For a well-calibrated quantile predictor, the coverage at quantile $\tau$ should equal $\tau$: i.e., $P(\text{actual} \leq \hat{q}_\tau) = \tau$.

\begin{figure}[ht]
\vskip 0.1in
\begin{center}
\centerline{\includegraphics[width=0.85\columnwidth]{images/online_calibration.png}}
\caption{Predictor calibration analysis. The histogram predictor achieves better calibration (closer to the diagonal), while EMA systematically over-estimates output length. Despite worse calibration, EMA achieves better routing performance.}
\label{fig:calibration}
\end{center}
\vskip -0.2in
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:calibration} reveals a surprising paradox. The histogram predictor is better calibrated: its q50 coverage is 56\% (close to the ideal 50\%), while EMA's q50 coverage is 72\% (significant over-estimation). Yet EMA achieves better routing performance (CR 1.18 vs 1.19).

\textbf{Key Finding 5: Better calibration does not imply better routing.} This paradox resolves when we consider the dynamics of online routing. EMA's over-estimation is actually beneficial: it sets a higher bar for routing to subscription, naturally implementing a conservative policy. More importantly, EMA adapts quickly to distribution shifts (common in real workloads), while histogram requires many samples per bin to update. For routing decisions, responsiveness trumps calibration.

\subsubsection{LP-Based Provider Mixing}

Given latency profiles, we solve for optimal routing fractions $\pi_j$ that minimize cost while satisfying a P99 SLO constraint $L$:
\begin{align}
\min_{\pi} \quad & \sum_j \pi_j c_j \nonumber \\
\text{s.t.} \quad & \sum_j \pi_j F_j(L) \geq 0.99 \label{eq:slo_constraint} \\
& \sum_j \pi_j = 1, \quad \pi_j \geq 0 \nonumber
\end{align}
where $F_j(L)$ is the empirical CDF of provider $j$ at latency $L$. By the simplex property, this LP yields at most 2 non-zero providers.

Figure~\ref{fig:latency_pareto} shows the Pareto frontier. At tight SLOs (2s), the optimal mix uses primarily Groq (fast but expensive). As SLO relaxes to 5s, traffic shifts to cheaper providers like Together and Parasail, reducing cost by 3$\times$ while still meeting the latency target.

\subsubsection{Smart Hedging for Tail Latency}

To provide tail latency guarantees without over-provisioning, we implement a \textit{smart hedging} mechanism. When a request's elapsed time exceeds a threshold where the expected remaining latency plus the fastest provider's expected latency would violate the SLO, we cancel the original request and retry on the fastest available provider.

The choice depends on application requirements: latency-critical applications may prefer aggressive hedging, while cost-sensitive applications can use residual-based hedging.
