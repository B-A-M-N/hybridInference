\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

We evaluate our proposed algorithms using real-world request traces to answer three key questions. First, what is the \textbf{economic potential} of optimal routing compared to naive baselines (Stage 1)? Second, when does \textbf{concurrency become a bottleneck}, and what is the ``cost of concurrency'' (Stage 2)? Third, can our learning-augmented algorithm approach the \textbf{offline optimal} performance under uncertainty?

\subsection{Experimental Setup}

\paragraph{Datasets.} We use four datasets with distinct characteristics, summarized in Table~\ref{tab:datasets}. \textbf{ShareGPT} contains 201K requests from single-turn conversations, representing a standard chatbot workload with moderate output lengths. \textbf{FreeInference} contains 371K requests with a multi-model mix, representing a diverse API gateway workload with high variance in both input and output. \textbf{RedNote} contains 55K requests from RAG-heavy applications with long contexts, representing an enterprise workload with the highest cost variance (coefficient of variation $> 2$). \textbf{BurstGPT} is a large-scale trace with 1.4M requests used specifically for online algorithm evaluation.

\paragraph{Baselines.} We compare against three baselines. \textbf{All-API} routes all requests to the pay-per-token API, serving as the cost upper bound. \textbf{Greedy (FCFS)} routes to subscription whenever capacity is available, regardless of request value. \textbf{Optimal (Offline)} computes the theoretical lower bound via our Stage 1/2 oracles.

\paragraph{Pricing Model.} We use the pricing of DeepSeek-R1 as a representative model: \$0.55/1M input tokens and \$2.19/1M output tokens. The subscription cost is amortized to define the break-even point.

\subsection{Predictor Calibration Analysis}

To understand why the simpler EMA predictor outperforms the histogram-based quantile predictor, we analyze their calibration properties. For a well-calibrated quantile predictor, the coverage at quantile $\tau$ should equal $\tau$: i.e., $P(\text{actual} \leq \hat{q}_\tau) = \tau$.

\begin{figure}[ht]
\vskip 0.1in
\begin{center}
\centerline{\includegraphics[width=0.85\columnwidth]{images/online_calibration.png}}
\caption{Predictor calibration analysis. The histogram predictor achieves better calibration (closer to the diagonal), while EMA systematically over-estimates output length. Despite worse calibration, EMA achieves better routing performance.}
\label{fig:calibration}
\end{center}
\vskip -0.2in
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:calibration} reveals a surprising paradox. The histogram predictor is better calibrated: its q50 coverage is 56\% (close to the ideal 50\%), while EMA's q50 coverage is 72\% (significant over-estimation). Yet EMA achieves better routing performance (CR 1.18 vs 1.19).

\textbf{Key Finding 5: Better calibration does not imply better routing.} This paradox resolves when we consider the dynamics of online routing. EMA's over-estimation is actually beneficial: it sets a higher bar for routing to subscription, naturally implementing a conservative policy. More importantly, EMA adapts quickly to distribution shifts (common in real workloads), while histogram requires many samples per bin to update. For routing decisions, responsiveness trumps calibration.

\subsection{Extension: Latency-Aware Routing}

While the preceding analysis focuses on cost optimization, practical deployments must also satisfy latency Service Level Objectives (SLOs). We extend our framework to incorporate latency constraints using real measurements from two providers serving GLM-4.6.

Interestingly, the Local GPU has better median latency (5.8s vs 14.0s) but worse tail latency (P99: 52.9s vs 49.1s), suggesting it experiences occasional queuing delays under load.

\textbf{LP Formulation.} Given a P99 SLO constraint $L$, we solve for the optimal routing fractions $\pi_j$ that minimize cost while ensuring the mixture CDF satisfies the SLO:
\begin{align}
\min_{\pi} \quad & \sum_j \pi_j c_j \nonumber \\
\text{s.t.} \quad & \sum_j \pi_j F_j(L) \geq 0.99 \label{eq:slo_constraint} \\
& \sum_j \pi_j = 1, \quad \pi_j \geq 0 \nonumber
\end{align}
where $F_j(L)$ is the empirical CDF of provider $j$ at latency $L$. This LP yields the cost-latency Pareto frontier.

\begin{figure}[ht]
\vskip 0.1in
\begin{center}
\centerline{\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{images/latency_pareto.png}}
\caption{Latency-aware routing for GLM-4.6. (a) Latency CDFs show Local GPU is faster on average but has longer tail. (b) LP routing achieves costs between the single-provider baselines. (c) At tight SLOs ($<$53s), the optimal mix uses 60\% Subscription to meet P99; as SLO relaxes, traffic shifts to free Local GPU.}
\label{fig:latency_pareto}
\end{center}
\vskip -0.2in
\end{figure}

\textbf{Key Finding 6: Provider mixing improves cost-latency tradeoffs.}
Figure~\ref{fig:latency_pareto} shows the Pareto frontier. At tight SLOs (e.g., 50s P99), the optimal routing uses 61\% Subscription and 39\% Local GPU, achieving a cost of \$0.08/1K requests---lower than Subscription-only (\$0.13/1K) while meeting the latency target. As the SLO relaxes beyond the Local GPU's P99 (53s), traffic shifts entirely to the free Local GPU.
